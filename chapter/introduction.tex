\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Background and Relevance}

Deterministic Finite Automata (DFA) are among the most fundamental computational models in computer science, used to represent and analyze systems with regular and predictable behavior. DFA minimization refers to the process of reducing the number of states in an automaton while keeping its language unchanged. This task is essential in many domains—such as compiler construction, text recognition, protocol verification, and software modeling—since smaller automata lead to more efficient storage, faster execution, and simpler analysis.

Traditionally, DFA minimization has been treated as a sequential problem, solved by well-known algorithms like Moore’s and Hopcroft’s methods. However, with today’s heavy reliance on parallel computing architectures, these sequential approaches fail to make full use of available hardware capabilities. Modern processors, especially Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), offer thousands of lightweight cores that can execute massive numbers of operations concurrently. Originally intended for rendering graphics, GPUs have evolved into a major platform for general-purpose computing (GPGPU), supporting scientific computation, artificial intelligence, and large-scale data analysis.

The growing computational capacity of GPUs opens a new line of research: how classical automata-theoretic algorithms can be reformulated for parallel execution. In particular, DFA minimization—being a well-structured yet computationally demanding problem—serves as an excellent testbed for evaluating the practical limits of massive parallelism.
This work investigates how different parallel algorithms for DFA minimization behave in practice and how theoretical efficiency translates to real GPU performance. The study not only benchmarks performance but also sheds light on the trade-offs between algorithmic complexity and hardware feasibility, an important issue for future parallel computing frameworks.

\section{Problem Statement}

Although DFA minimization belongs to \textbf{Nick’s Class (NC)}—meaning it can, in theory, be computed efficiently in parallel—most proposed algorithms struggle to perform well on real hardware. Approaches that achieve the best asymptotic time bounds, such as those based on computing a \textbf{complete transitive closure}, require enormous parallel resources and memory, making them impractical for large inputs.

Algorithms based on \textbf{partition refinement}, on the other hand, have higher theoretical time complexity but typically perform better in practice because they map more naturally onto GPU hardware and memory structures. Yet, even among these, efficiency varies:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Leader-based parallel refinement} is conceptually simple but may require many iterations.
  \item \textit{Sorting-based refinement} can minimize iterations but increases per-iteration workload.
  \item \textit{Hybrid algorithms} that incorporate partial transitive closure steps can speed up convergence for certain automata but do not always generalize across datasets.
\end{itemize}

The core issue this research addresses is therefore the \textbf{gap between theoretical parallel complexity and practical performance}. The paper evaluates several GPU-based DFA minimization algorithms to determine:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why algorithms with superior theoretical complexity often fail to scale effectively on GPUs.
  \item Which variant of partition refinement best utilizes GPU parallelism.
  \item Whether combining partial closure computation with refinement can consistently improve execution time.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}

The overarching goal is to implement and assess massively parallel algorithms for DFA minimization using GPU architectures. The work sets out to meet the following specific objectives:
\begin{description}
  \item[Implementation:] Develop GPU-based versions of multiple DFA minimization algorithms—including transitive-closure-based and partition-refinement-based methods—using CUDA C++.
  \item[Experimental Evaluation:] Compare the algorithms over diverse benchmark families such as Fibonacci automata, Bit-Splitter automata, and the VLTS benchmark suite, analyzing runtime, scalability, and memory efficiency.
  \item[Performance Analysis:] Examine how theoretical complexity relates to observed performance on GPUs, identifying cases where simpler algorithms outperform more complex ones.
  \item[Hybrid Optimization:] Explore the impact of introducing a partial transitive closure phase before refinement to accelerate convergence for particular automata structures.
  \item[Practical Contribution:] Identify which approaches most effectively exploit GPU parallelism and provide insights for future research on parallel automata-processing systems.
\end{description}

Ultimately, the study aims to bridge the space between abstract parallel complexity theory and real-world GPU implementations, advancing the design of scalable, high-performance solutions for automata minimization and similar computational problems.
